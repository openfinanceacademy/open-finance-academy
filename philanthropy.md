## What philanthropy means

Imagine a neighbor who tutors students each week, a family that supports a food pantry, and a group that organizes help after a flood. Each contributes something toward other people's wellbeing.

**[Philanthropy]({{ '/glossary/' | relative_url }}#philanthropy)** is voluntary giving of money, time, skills, or other resources to improve people's lives and support the common good. A **[philanthropist]({{ '/glossary/' | relative_url }}#philanthropist)** is someone who takes part in that giving. Wealth and fame are not requirements; Fidelity Charitable explicitly includes people who contribute experience or volunteer their time. [Source: Fidelity Charitable](https://www.fidelitycharitable.org/guidance/philanthropy/what-is-a-philanthropist.html)

The word's Greek roots express love of humankind. Michael Layton, writing for the Johnson Center for Philanthropy, argues that identifying philanthropy mainly with large financial gifts overlooks everyday generosity and relationships within communities. His broader view includes neighbors helping one another and people organizing support together. [Source: Johnson Center](https://johnsoncenter.org/blog/defining-philanthropy-part-i-love-of-humankind/)

## Immediate help and lasting change

Giving can address a need today or support work intended to prevent that need in the future. These purposes can overlap. CAF describes philanthropy as including both one-off gifts and longer-term efforts to address social problems. [Source: Charities Aid Foundation](https://www.cafonline.org/personal-giving/resources/what-is-philanthropy)

Consider these invented examples:

| Community concern | Help with an immediate need | Work toward lasting change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hunger | Support meals for families this week. | Support a community-led project to improve reliable access to food. |
| Education | Provide supplies requested by a classroom. | Volunteer regularly with a reading program. |
| Flooding | Contribute requested emergency supplies. | Support local flood-preparation work. |

People sometimes use “charity” for immediate relief and “philanthropy” for longer-term change. Treat that as a useful distinction between purposes, not a strict boundary: the Johnson Center traces this historical distinction while arguing for a wider understanding of philanthropy. A meal today and prevention work can both matter. [Source: Johnson Center](https://johnsoncenter.org/blog/defining-philanthropy-part-i-love-of-humankind/)

## Ways to contribute

Start with what a community or organization says it needs and what you can realistically offer.

| Contribution | Example | A useful question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Money | Make a single gift or a recurring contribution. | What work will this support? |
| Time | Help at a community garden or food pantry. | What schedule and training does the role require? |
| Skills | Offer translation, bookkeeping, or website help. | Has the organization asked for this skill? |
| Goods | Give requested supplies or equipment. | Can the recipient use, store, and maintain these items? |
| Shared giving | Pool money with others for an agreed cause. | How will the group choose recipients? |

A **[giving circle]({{ '/glossary/' | relative_url }}#giving-circle)** is a group whose members pool resources and decide together which causes or organizations to support. Fidelity Charitable describes giving circles alongside individual gifts and volunteering as ways to participate. [Source: Fidelity Charitable](https://www.fidelitycharitable.org/guidance/philanthropy/what-is-a-philanthropist.html)

CAF also describes family philanthropy, where relatives develop a shared practice of giving, and strategic philanthropy, where a donor chooses causes and plans how to support them. A simple plan can start with one cause, one manageable commitment, and a date to review it. [Source: Charities Aid Foundation](https://www.cafonline.org/personal-giving/resources/what-is-philanthropy)

## A worked giving plan

A **[budget]({{ '/glossary/' | relative_url }}#budget)** is a plan for how to use the money available over a stated period, including spending, saving, and debt payments. Giving can be one part of that plan. See the [household budgeting lesson]({{ '/knowledge/personal-finance-and-financial-wellbeing/' | relative_url }}#make-a-plan-for-the-money-coming-in).

**Example assumptions:** All amounts and people below are invented. Sam has chosen to set aside $30 per month for giving after allowing for other household commitments. The plan covers 12 months, assumes no fees or tax effects, and earns no interest. Volunteer hours are tracked separately, with no dollar value assigned to them.

Sam wants to support local food access while leaving room for unexpected community needs.

| Use | Calculation | Annual amount |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Monthly food pantry gifts | $20 × 12 months | $240 |
| Money set aside for later giving | $10 × 12 months | $120 |
| Total giving budget | $30 × 12 months | $360 |

The amounts balance: **$240 + $120 = $360**. The $120 is still money Sam has set aside, not a completed gift. If Sam later donates $75 of it to an emergency appeal, **$120 − $75 = $45** remains available, and gifts made total **$240 + $75 = $315** for the year.

Sam also volunteers two hours each month: **2 × 12 = 24 hours a year**. Those hours describe a separate contribution; they do not increase the $360 cash budget. This is an illustration of planning, not a suggested giving amount for everyone.

## Ask about results and decision-making

EBSCO's overview describes a sector ranging from small volunteer groups to large institutions. It also discusses how personal connections and emotional stories can influence giving, and how very large donors can shape public priorities. These are reasons to ask both what the work achieves and whose needs guide it. [Source: EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/economics/philanthropy)

Use these questions when considering a contribution:

- **Who identified the need?** Look for a clear role for people affected by the issue.
- **What will the contribution make possible?** Ask for a concrete description of the work.
- **What would improvement look like?** Distinguish the work performed from changes in people's lives.
- **What has the organization learned?** Look for explanations of progress, setbacks, and adjustments.
- **Who makes decisions?** Ask how recipients and community members can give feedback.

For example, an invented tutoring project might report 100 sessions delivered. That tells you how much activity took place. To understand whether students benefited, you would also want evidence about their learning and feedback from students and families. The session count alone cannot establish that learning improved or that tutoring caused any improvement.

These questions are a starting point for reflection, not a scoring system. A small neighborhood group and a large institution may have different ways of sharing evidence. The Johnson Center's emphasis on everyday giving is a reminder to pay attention to relationships as well as money totals. [Source: Johnson Center](https://johnsoncenter.org/blog/defining-philanthropy-part-i-love-of-humankind/)

## Check your understanding

1. A person gives no money but regularly volunteers to translate community information. Can that be philanthropy?
2. In Sam's example, how much has been donated after the $75 emergency gift, and how much remains set aside?
3. Five friends each contribute $15 per month to a giving circle for six months. Assuming no fees or interest, how much do they pool?
4. A tutoring project reports 100 sessions. Does that alone show that students learned more?

### Answers

1. **Yes.** Giving useful time and skills to support others falls within the broad meaning of philanthropy.
2. **$315 donated and $45 remaining.** The pantry receives $240, and the emergency appeal receives $75. Together they use $315 of the $360 annual plan.
3. **$450.** Five people × $15 = $75 per month; $75 × 6 months = $450. Pooling contributions lets the group make a shared decision about that total.
4. **No.** Sessions measure activity. Evidence about learning is needed to judge the result, and other factors may also affect students' progress.
